\subsection{Backend Performance}
\label{sec:eval_backend}

Backend performance is evaluated with k6 against the deployed system. The methodology deliberately replaces the read-only, single-endpoint scenarios of the specialized project with a multi-user mixed read/write profile tied directly to NFR-2.1, and the evaluation doubles as a remediation loop: three deployment configurations are measured in sequence, each bottleneck found is fixed, and the fix is verified by re-running the identical load.

\subsubsection{Test Environment}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Server}: development machine; PM2-managed Uvicorn serving the FastAPI application, with PostgreSQL 16 (pgvector), Redis 7, Neo4j 5, Garage S3 and LiveKit in Docker
    \item \textbf{Load generator}: k6 v2.3 running from a separate machine on the same LAN, over HTTPS to the production domain --- TLS termination included, unlike the previous iteration's plain-HTTP LAN runs
    \item \textbf{Authentication}: 200 synthetic students seeded on the deployment (organisation membership, course enrolment, and 30-day JWTs minted with the application's own signing key), so every virtual user authenticates as a \emph{distinct} student; Google OAuth remains out of scope as an external dependency
    \item \textbf{Configurations measured}, in remediation order:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Config A} --- one Uvicorn worker, PostgreSQL at its default \texttt{max\_connections=100} (the specialized-project deployment)
        \item \textbf{Config B} --- four Uvicorn workers (identical in every other respect)
        \item \textbf{Config C} --- four workers with PostgreSQL \texttt{max\_connections=300}
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Test Scenarios}

The peak profile models a live class session. Each level sustains for three minutes with a 2\,s think time:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Browse (80\% of virtual users)}: profile bootstrap, enrolled-course list, notification-badge polling, course progress, spaced-repetition state, and curriculum pages --- the per-request pattern of the learner dashboard and course-learn views.
    \item \textbf{Quiz taking (20\%)}: the full write journey against one published quiz --- start attempt, per-question answer writes (each firing the SM-2 review in-transaction), session heartbeats, integrity-event batches, and final submission with grading. This is precisely the write path and the concurrent-attempts-on-one-quiz case the previous evaluation did not represent.
    \item \textbf{Multiplier matrix}: $1\times$ (50 virtual users --- the NFR-2.1 target), $2\times$ (100) and $4\times$ (200), to evidence headroom rather than a single point.
\end{itemize}

Because every virtual user holds a distinct student token, concurrent attempts contend as they would between real students; the single-session guard is exercised rather than tripped artificially by a shared identity.

Two clauses of NFR-2.1 are deliberately outside this evaluation and are treated as future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion_future_work}): the ten simultaneous video streams, because no media material is published on the deployment to stream; and the five concurrent AI interview sessions, because each session consumes LLM gateway tokens and enqueues evaluation jobs, making load generation a cost decision rather than a measurement one. The evaluation therefore evidences the concurrent-active-users clause of the NFR over the HTTP control plane.

\subsubsection{Analysis against NFR Targets}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck progression (evidence-driven remediation).} Under Config A the throughput ceiling is 35--37 requests per second regardless of offered load. Server telemetry during the $4\times$ run shows the single Uvicorn process at 110\% CPU (one core) while the machine idles at 71.8\% and PostgreSQL sits at 10\% --- the process model, not the database, was the wall. Moving to four workers (Config B) lifted the ceiling to 111--113 requests per second ($\approx 3\times$, near-linear per worker) and cut p50 at the NFR level from 661\,ms to 73\,ms ($9\times$). At $4\times$ overload, Config B then surfaced the \emph{next} bottleneck: 0.55\% of requests failed with HTTP 500 traced to \texttt{psycopg.OperationalError} --- PostgreSQL rejecting connections because four workers ($\times$ pool 10 + overflow 10 each) plus the background worker and agents exhausted the default \texttt{max\_connections=100}. Raising it to 300 (Config C) eliminated the failures: the identical $4\times$ run reports 0.00\% errors and zero HTTP 500s, with throughput unchanged ($\approx$111 RPS) because the remaining saturation is worker CPU, not connection scarcity.
    \item \textbf{NFR-2.1: concurrent-active-users clause satisfied at target, with measured headroom.} At the NFR's own level (50 concurrent active users, driven harder than human pacing at a 2\,s think time), the final configuration sustains 111 requests per second with p50 73\,ms, p95 464\,ms, 0\% errors and a 1.67\,s maximum. The NFR level consumes roughly 45\% of measured capacity; latency begins degrading near $2\times$, and the first request failure appears only at $4\times$ --- and disappears again once the connection ceiling is lifted. The video-stream and AI-interview-session clauses of the NFR remain untested for the reasons stated above.
    \item \textbf{NFR-1.4 (dashboard within 2\,s) and NFR-2.2 (queries within 0.5\,s).} Both pass at the NFR level under the final configuration: every endpoint's p50 is below 400\,ms and the slowest observed request is 1.67\,s. At $2\times$ the aggregate average remains near 0.5\,s; at $4\times$ both are exceeded --- degradation beyond design load is graceful (queueing) rather than failure.
    \item \textbf{TC-NFR-2.1 (excess requests handled without crashing).} Across every configuration and level --- including $4\times$ the target on a single development machine --- no process crashed, no restart occurred, and the sole failure mode observed (database connection exhaustion) was bounded (0.55\%), diagnosed, and removed by configuration.
    \item \textbf{Comparison with the specialized-project results.} The previous iteration reported 136--431 requests per second on single-endpoint authenticated GETs; the two figures are not comparable. The earlier profile exercised shared, cacheable read paths with one identity, whereas the final profile is per-user (200 distinct students, cold per-user caches) and includes the quiz write journey with in-transaction SM-2 scheduling. The final numbers are the more conservative and the more realistic of the two.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{AI Model Comparison for Quiz Generation}
\label{sec:eval_ai_model}

The quiz generation pipeline supports multiple LLM backends via the LLM Gateway abstraction. This section evaluates model performance for the quiz generation task across quality, latency, and cost dimensions to determine the optimal production default.

\subsubsection{Methodology}

Three candidate models were compared on a benchmark of two lessons from the \textit{Data Warehousing \& Data Mining} course (Chapter~1: Overview, Chapter~2: Basic Issues), running each model 3~times $\times$ 12~questions $\times$ 2~lessons = 72 expected questions per model. A JSON-repair fallback (via \texttt{gpt-5-chat-latest} with a structure-only system prompt) recovers malformed outputs, bringing all models to full sample size ($n = 72$ for gpt-oss and gemma, $n = 77$ for llama due to over-generation).

\paragraph{Generation parameters}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Temperature $= 0.3$, top\_p $= 0.95$
    \item 12 questions per lesson, fixed Bloom taxonomy mix (60\% remember, 30\% understand, 10\% apply)
    \item Same prompt template, same retrieved chunks, same JSON schema across all candidates
    \item Direct LLM gateway call (bypassing ARQ) to isolate the model effect from queue latency
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Judge protocol}
An independent judge model (\texttt{gpt-5-chat-latest}) evaluated each generated question using a \textbf{reference-guided chain-of-thought} protocol~\cite{zheng2023llmjudge}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Independently solve} the question using only the source chunks.
    \item \textbf{Compare} the independent answer with the marked-correct option and the candidate's explanation.
    \item \textbf{Score} the rubric only after Steps 1--2.
\end{enumerate}

This protocol reduces judge failure rates from 14/20 (default prompt) $\to$ 6/20 (plain CoT) $\to$ 3/20 (reference-guided), as reported in Table~4 of Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023llmjudge}. Anti-bias guardrails counter position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases catalogued in~\cite{zheng2023llmjudge,li2024llmjudgesurvey}.

The judge scores each question on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Groundedness} (strict: explicit textual support required from source chunks)
    \item \textbf{Answer correctness} (does the marked-correct option match the source material?)
    \item \textbf{Judge-solve match} (fraction where the judge's independent answer matches the marked-correct option --- structural reliability check)
    \item \textbf{Distractor plausibility} (1--5 scale: 1 = trivially eliminable, 5 = requires deep understanding to distinguish)
    \item \textbf{Overall quality} (1--10 single-answer scale per Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023llmjudge} Figure~6)
    \item \textbf{Schema compliance} (valid JSON output parseable by the pipeline)
\end{itemize}

Confidence intervals are 95\% non-parametric bootstrap (2,000 resamples at question level). Decoding: temperature $= 0.0$, greedy (standard for LLM-as-a-judge stability~\cite{zheng2023llmjudge}).

\subsubsection{Failure Analysis}

Each model exhibits distinct failure patterns under the reference-guided judge:

\paragraph{gpt-oss-120b} (production default)
Primary failure mode is \textit{paraphrase-depth}: the model rephrases source material at a level the judge's Step-1 solve cannot independently reproduce from the chunks alone ($\approx$5.6\% of questions). These are not factual hallucinations --- the judge's groundedness and solve-match failures overlap perfectly, confirming the issue is inference depth rather than content drift.

\paragraph{gemma-4-31b-it} (highest fidelity)
`Zero quality failures across all dimensions. The trade-off is distractor plausibility (3.86 vs gpt-oss's 4.07): gemma writes distractors that are \textit{too obviously wrong} for a learner familiar with the source, reducing exam discrimination power. One raw output required JSON structural repair (trailing prose after closing brace), recovered by the repair pass.

\paragraph{llama-3.3-70b} (not recommended for production)
Exhibits \textit{external-knowledge contamination} in $\approx$22\% of questions: the model injects pre-training knowledge (e.g., naming specific vendors not in the source), overgeneralises concepts beyond source scope, or confabulates examples. The reference-guided judge correctly identifies these because its Step-1 solve fails to land on llama's marked-correct option.

\subsubsection{Recommendation}

\textbf{Production default: gpt-oss-120b.} Fastest (4$\times$), cheapest (2.2$\times$), schema-perfect, highest distractor plausibility. Its 94.4\% strict groundedness approaches gemma's 100\% under reference-guided judging --- the gap is paraphrasing style, not factual error.

\textbf{High-stakes fallback: gemma-4-31b-it.} For graded assessments where any ungrounded content is unacceptable, gemma's perfect fidelity justifies the latency and cost premium. Schema fragility is mitigated by the JSON-repair pipeline.

All three models under-deliver on Bloom's \texttt{apply}-level questions (target 10\%, actual $<$2\%). This is addressable at the prompt level with worked examples, independent of model choice.
